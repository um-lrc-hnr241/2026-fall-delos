01 / Context

Introduction

What would make an artificial being’s experience count as a real inner life?

Two worlds, one enduring question

Plutarch (c. 46–after 119 CE) was a Greek author and philosopher whose works address ethics, religion, politics, and the limits of knowledge. He did not write about machine consciousness. Reading him beside Westworld is a way to ask how older philosophical questions can illuminate a modern story—not to claim that Plutarch anticipated artificial intelligence.

In Westworld, hosts are designed beings whose memories and behavior are shaped by their creators. As the series unfolds, the hosts’ accounts of experience challenge the boundary between programmed response and conscious life. Their stories make viewers ask whether consciousness depends on a human origin, or whether signs such as memory, feeling, and self-reflection matter more.

A careful comparison

Plutarch’s essays and dialogues offer varied discussions of the soul, religious experience, and human understanding. They do not present one simple test for consciousness that can be directly applied to a fictional host. Our comparison instead uses his writings as a prompt to think about what can be known about another being’s inner life, and how ethical responsibilities follow from that uncertainty.

Our guiding question is: When a being remembers, suffers, and understands itself through a story, what reasons do we have to recognize its consciousness?
